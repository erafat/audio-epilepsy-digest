What Counts as Neuromodulation Success?

Self-assessment on responder thresholds, denominators and time horizons, patient-important outcomes, and research-stage selection biomarkers.

Learning Objectives

  1. Explain why the 50 percent responder rate remains useful but cannot serve as a complete clinical verdict.
  2. Interpret neuromodulation outcomes using seizure freedom, continuous reduction, trajectory, denominator, function, treatment burden, harms, and durability.
  3. Distinguish exploratory biomarker associations and internal held-out testing from externally validated patient-selection tools.

Questions

1. What is the most defensible role for the 50 percent responder threshold in neuromodulation studies?
Correct answer: B. The episode retains the familiar threshold for communication and comparison while denying it solitary primacy or universal surrogate status.
2. A study reports a higher responder proportion at later visits. What must be checked before describing cumulative benefit?
Correct answer: C. Changing denominators, attrition, time-varying cointerventions, programming, and absent controls can make later observed proportions look different without proving a cumulative device effect.
3. How should the ESTEL result be summarized?
Correct answer: B. The episode preserves both the negative prespecified primary result and the positive objective secondary signal without treating objective detection and patient importance as synonyms.
4. Which finding would be sufficient to use a biomarker for current neuromodulation candidate selection?
Correct answer: C. Prediction development, external validation, calibration, and decision utility are separate steps. The episode treats the cited EEG and inflammatory findings as hypothesis-generating.
